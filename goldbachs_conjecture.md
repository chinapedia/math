title: Goldbach's conjecture
---

**Goldbach's conjecture** is one of the oldest and best-known [unsolved problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/list_of_unsolved_problems_in_mathematics)s in [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/number_theory) and all of [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/mathematics). It states that every [even](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/even_and_odd_numbers) [natural number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/natural_number) greater than 2 is the sum of two [prime number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/prime_number)s.

The conjecture has been shown to hold for all natural numbers less than 4e18, but remains [unproven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/mathematical_proof) despite considerable effort.

## History

### Origins

On 7 June 1742, the [Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussia)n mathematician [Christian Goldbach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Goldbach) wrote a letter to [Leonhard Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonhard_Euler) (letter XLIII),[^1] in which he proposed the following conjecture:

> Every integer that can be written as the sum of two primes can also be written as the sum of as many primes (including unity) as one wishes, until all terms are units.

Goldbach was following the now-abandoned convention of [considering 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number#Primality_of_one) to be a [prime number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/prime_number),[^2] so that a sum of units would be a sum of primes. He then proposed a second conjecture in the margin of his letter, which implies the first:

> It seems at least, that every integer greater than 2 can be written as the sum of three primes.

Euler replied in a letter dated 30 June 1742[^3] and reminded Goldbach of an earlier conversation they had had ("... so Ew. vormals mit mir communicirt haben ..."), in which Goldbach had remarked that the first of those two conjectures would follow from the statement

This is in fact equivalent to his second, marginal conjecture. In the letter dated 30 June 1742, Euler stated:[^4] [^5]

> That ... every even integer is a sum of two primes, I regard as a completely certain theorem, although I cannot prove it.

### Weaker Descartes' conjecture

[René Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ren%C3%A9_Descartes) wrote, "Every even number can be expressed as the sum of at most three primes."[^6] This proposition is similar to, but weaker than, Goldbach's conjecture. [Paul Erdős](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Erd%C5%91s) said, "Descartes actually discovered this before Goldbach ... but it is better that the conjecture was named for Goldbach because, mathematically speaking, Descartes was infinitely rich and Goldbach was very poor."[^7]

### Partial results

Goldbach's conjecture involving the sum of two primes is much more difficult than the [weak Goldbach conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/weak_Goldbach_conjecture), which says that every odd integer greater than 5 is the sum of three primes. Using [Vinogradov's method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Vinogradov#Mathematical_contributions), [Nikolai Chudakov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Chudakov),[^8] [Johannes van der Corput](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_van_der_Corput),[^9] and [Theodor Estermann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Estermann)[^10] showed (1937–1938) that [almost all](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/almost_all) even numbers can be written as the sum of two primes (in the sense that the fraction of even numbers up to some $N$ which can be so written tends towards 1 as $N$ increases). In 1930, [Lev Schnirelmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lev_Schnirelmann) proved that any [natural number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/natural_number) greater than 1 can be written as the sum of not more than $C$ prime numbers, where $C$ is an effectively computable constant; see [Schnirelmann density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schnirelmann_density).[^11] [^12] Schnirelmann's constant is the lowest number $C$ with this property. Schnirelmann himself obtained $C < 800,000$. This result was subsequently enhanced by many authors, such as [Olivier Ramaré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olivier_Ramar%C3%A9), who in 1995 showed that every even number $n ≥ 4$ is in fact the sum of at most 6 primes. The best known result stems from the proof of the weak Goldbach conjecture by [Harald Helfgott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harald_Helfgott),[^13] which directly implies that every even number $n ≥ 4$ is the sum of at most 4 primes.[^14] [^15]

In 1924, [Hardy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._H._Hardy) and [Littlewood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Edensor_Littlewood) showed[^16] under the assumption of the [generalized Riemann hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/generalized_Riemann_hypothesis) that the number of even numbers up to $X$ violating the Goldbach conjecture is [much less than](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inequality_%28mathematics%29) $X^{ + c}$ for small $c$.[^17]

In 1948, using [sieve theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/sieve_theory) methods, [Alfréd Rényi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfr%C3%A9d_R%C3%A9nyi) showed that every sufficiently large even number can be written as the sum of a prime and an [almost prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/almost_prime) with at most $K$ factors.[^18] [Chen Jingrun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chen_Jingrun) showed in 1973 using sieve theory that every [sufficiently large](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/sufficiently_large) even number can be written as the sum of either two primes, or a prime and a [semiprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/semiprime) (the product of two primes).[^19] See [Chen's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chen%27s_theorem) for further information.

In 1975, [Hugh Lowell Montgomery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Lowell_Montgomery) and [Bob Vaughan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Vaughan) showed that "most" even numbers are expressible as the sum of two primes. More precisely, they showed that there exist positive constants $c$ and $C$ such that for all sufficiently large numbers $N$, every even number less than $N$ is the sum of two primes, with at most $CN^{1 − c}$ exceptions. In particular, the set of even integers that are not the sum of two primes has [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/natural_density) zero.

In 1951, [Yuri Linnik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuri_Linnik) proved the existence of a constant $K$ such that every sufficiently large even number is the sum of two primes and at most $K$ powers of 2. [János Pintz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%A1nos_Pintz) and [Imre Ruzsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imre_Z._Ruzsa) found in 2020 that $K = 8$ works.[^20] Assuming the [generalized Riemann hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/generalized_Riemann_hypothesis), $K = 7$ also works, as shown by [Roger Heath-Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Heath-Brown) and [Jan-Christoph Schlage-Puchta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan-Christoph_Schlage-Puchta) in 2002.[^21]

In 2013, [Harald Helfgott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harald_Helfgott) submitted a proof of the weak conjecture to the *[Annals of Mathematics Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annals_of_Mathematics_Studies)* book series. The article was accepted, but Helfgott decided to undertake major modifications suggested by the referee.[^22] [^23] [^24] The weak conjecture is implied by the Goldbach conjecture, as if $n − 3$ is a sum of two primes, then $n$ is a sum of three primes. But the converse implication and thus the Goldbach conjecture remain unproven even if Helfgott's proof is correct.

### Computational results

For small values of $n$, the Goldbach conjecture (and hence the weak Goldbach conjecture) can be verified directly. For instance, in 1938, [Nils Pipping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nils_Pipping) laboriously verified the conjecture up to $n = 100,000$.[^25] With the advent of computers, many more values of $n$ have been checked; [Tomás Oliveira e Silva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom%C3%A1s_Oliveira_e_Silva) ran a distributed computer search that has verified the conjecture for $n ≤ 4e18$ (and double-checked up to 4e17) as of 2013. One record from this search is that 3,325,581,707,333,960,528 is the smallest number that cannot be written as a sum of two primes where one is smaller than 9781.[^26]

The modern version is thus probably stronger (but in order to confirm that, one would have to prove that the first version, freely applied to any positive even integer $n$, could not possibly rule out the existence of such a specific counterexample $N$). In any case, the modern statements have the same relationships with each other as the older statements did. That is, the second and third modern statements are equivalent, and either implies the first modern statement.

The third modern statement (equivalent to the second) is the form in which the conjecture is usually expressed today. Statistical considerations that focus on the [probabilistic distribution of prime numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/prime_number_theorem) present informal evidence in favour of the conjecture (in both the weak and strong forms) for [sufficiently large](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/sufficiently_large) integers: the greater the integer, the more ways there are available for that number to be represented as the sum of two or three other numbers and the more "likely" it becomes that at least one of these representations consists entirely of primes.

A very crude version of the [heuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/heuristic) probabilistic argument (for the strong form of the Goldbach conjecture) is as follows. The [prime number theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/prime_number_theorem) asserts that an integer $m$ selected at random has roughly a $1/ln m$ chance of being prime. Thus if $n$ is a large even integer and $m$ is a number between 3 and $n/2$, then one might expect the probability of $m$ and $n − m$ simultaneously being prime to be $1/ln m ln(n − m)$. If one pursues this heuristic, one might expect the total number of ways to write a large even integer $n$ as the sum of two odd primes to be roughly

$$
\sum_{m=3}^\frac{n}{2} \frac{1}{\ln m} \frac{1}{\ln(n - m)} \approx \frac{n}{2 (\ln n)^2}.
$$

Since $\ln n\ll\sqrt{n}$, this quantity goes to infinity as $n$ increases, and one would expect that every large even integer has not just one representation as the sum of two primes, but in fact very many such representations.

This heuristic argument is actually somewhat inaccurate because it assumes that the events of $m$ and $n − m$ being prime are [statistically independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/statistical_independence) of each other. For instance, if $m$ is odd, then $n − m$ is also odd, and if $m$ is even, then $n − m$ is even, a non-trivial relation because, besides the number 2, only odd numbers can be prime. Similarly, if $n$ is divisible by 3, and $m$ was already a prime other than 3, then $n − m$ would also be [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/coprime) to 3 and thus be slightly more likely to be prime than a general number. Pursuing this type of analysis more carefully, [G. H. Hardy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._H._Hardy) and [John Edensor Littlewood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Edensor_Littlewood) in 1923 conjectured (as part of their *[Hardy–Littlewood prime tuple conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_prime#First_Hardy%E2%80%93Littlewood_conjecture)*) that for any fixed $c ≥ 2$, the number of representations of a large integer $n$ as the sum of $c$ primes $n = p_1 + ⋯ + p_c$ with $p_1 ≤ ⋯ ≤ p_c$ should be [asymptotically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/asymptotic_analysis) equal to

$$
\left(\prod_p \frac{p \gamma_{c,p}(n)}{(p - 1)^c}\right)
 \int_{2 \leq x_1 \leq \cdots \leq x_c: x_1 + \cdots + x_c = n} \frac{dx_1 \cdots dx_{c-1}}{\ln x_1 \cdots \ln x_c},
$$

where the product is over all primes $p$, and $γ_{c,p}(n)$ is the number of solutions to the equation $n = q_1 + ⋯ + q_c mod p$ in [modular arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/modular_arithmetic), subject to the [constraints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constraint_%28mathematics%29) $q_1, ..., q_c ≠ 0 mod p$. This formula has been rigorously proven to be asymptotically valid for $c ≥ 3$ from the work of [Ivan Matveevich Vinogradov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Matveevich_Vinogradov), but is still only a conjecture when $c = 2$. In the latter case, the above formula simplifies to 0 when $n$ is odd, and to

$$
2 \Pi_2 \left(\prod_{p \mid n; p \geq 3} \frac{p - 1}{p - 2}\right) \int_2^n \frac{dx}{(\ln x)^2}
 \approx 2 \Pi_2 \left(\prod_{p \mid n; p \geq 3} \frac{p - 1}{p - 2}\right) \frac{n}{(\ln n)^2}
$$

when $n$ is even, where $Π_2$ is [Hardy–Littlewood's twin prime constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_prime#First_Hardy%E2%80%93Littlewood_conjecture)

$$
\Pi_2 := \prod_{p\;{\rm prime} \ge 3} \left(1 - \frac{1}{(p-1)^2}\right) \approx 0.66016\,18158\,46869\,57392\,78121\,10014\dots
$$

This is sometimes known as the *extended Goldbach conjecture*. The Goldbach conjecture is in fact very similar to the [twin prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/twin_prime) conjecture, and the two conjectures are believed to be of roughly comparable difficulty.

The * function* associates to each even integer the number of ways it can be decomposed into a sum of two primes. Its graph looks like a [comet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/comet) and is therefore called [Goldbach's comet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldbach%27s_comet).[^27]

Goldbach's comet suggests tight upper and lower bounds on the number of representations of an even number as the sum of two primes, and also that the number of these representations depends strongly on the value modulo 3 of the number.

## Related problems

Although Goldbach's conjecture implies that every positive integer greater than one can be written as a sum of at most three primes, it is not always possible to find such a sum using a [greedy algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/greedy_algorithm) that uses the largest possible prime at each step. The [Pillai sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pillai_sequence) tracks the numbers requiring the largest number of primes in their greedy representations.[^28]

Similar problems to Goldbach's conjecture exist in which primes are replaced by other particular sets of numbers, such as the squares:
* [Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph-Louis_Lagrange) proved that [every positive integer is the sum of four squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange%27s_four-square_theorem). See [Waring's problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waring%27s_problem) and the related [Waring–Goldbach problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waring%E2%80%93Goldbach_problem) on sums of powers of primes.
* Hardy and Littlewood listed as their Conjecture I: "Every large odd number ( $n > 5$) is the sum of a prime and the double of a prime".[^29] This conjecture is known as [Lemoine's conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lemoine%27s_conjecture) and is also called *Levy's conjecture*.
* The Goldbach conjecture for [practical number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/practical_number)s, a prime-like sequence of integers, was stated by Margenstern in 1984,[^30] and proved by [Melfi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giuseppe_Melfi) in 1996:[^31] every even number is a sum of two practical numbers.
* [Harvey Dubner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvey_Dubner) proposed a strengthening of the Goldbach conjecture: that every even integer greater than 4208 is the sum of two [twin prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/twin_prime)s (not necessarily belonging to the same pair).[^32] Only 34 even integers less than 4208 are not the sum of two twin primes; Dubner has verified computationally that this list is complete up to $2\cdot 10^{10}.$[^33] A proof of this stronger conjecture would imply not only Goldbach's conjecture but also the [twin prime conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/twin_prime_conjecture).

Goldbach's conjecture is used to study computational complexity.[^34] The connection is made through the [Busy Beaver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Busy_Beaver) function, where BB(*n*) is the maximum number of steps taken by any *n* state [Turing machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_machine) that halts. There is a 27-state Turing machine that halts if and only if Goldbach's conjecture is false.[^34] Hence if BB(27) was known, and the Turing machine did not stop in that number of steps, it would be known to run forever and hence no counterexamples would exist (proving the conjecture). This is a completely impractical way to settle the conjecture; instead it is used to suggest that BB(27) will be very hard to compute, at least as difficult as settling the Goldbach conjecture.

## In popular culture

*Goldbach's Conjecture* () is the title of [Xu Chi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xu_Chi)'s biography of Chinese mathematician and number theorist [Chen Jingrun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chen_Jingrun).

The conjecture is a central point in the plot of [Apostolos Doxiadis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostolos_Doxiadis)'s 1992 novel *[Uncle Petros and Goldbach's Conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncle_Petros_and_Goldbach%27s_Conjecture)*, in [Isaac Asimov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Asimov)'s short story "[Sixty Million Trillion Combinations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sixty_Million_Trillion_Combinations)", and in [Michelle Richmond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelle_Richmond)'s 2008 mystery novel *No One You Know*.[^35]

Goldbach's conjecture is featured as the main topic of research of the eponymous character Marguerite in the 2023 French-Swiss film *[Marguerite's Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marguerite%27s_Theorem)*.[^36]
